Paw Pages v1

A web app where a handler keeps a written record for each of their pets — profile, vaccinations, and everything else worth logging — and can hand out a public page for any one of them.

Status  Scope signed off · ready to build Stack  React · FastAPI · Supabase Screens  8, desktop and mobile Schema  Already live, unchanged
What this PRD closes

Q20b is answered: the ten exclusions stay out. v1 is exactly the eight screens already drawn in design/ and the three tables already migrated in supabase/migrations/. No reminders, no schedules, no charts, no attachments.

The stack is decided: React frontend in web/, FastAPI backend in backend/. The browser talks only to our backend — never to Postgres, Supabase Auth or Supabase Storage directly.

Problem Statement

The problem the handler is facing, from the handler's perspective.

A person who keeps pets accumulates a record that lives nowhere. The rabies certificate is in a drawer, the deworming date is in a WhatsApp message to the vet, the grooming appointment is a memory, and the booster that was due in July is a thing they meant to think about. The vet's paper card covers only the shots, only for as long as it survives, and only for one animal at a time.

Three specific failures follow. Things get missed — nothing anywhere says a booster is forty-six days overdue, and the handler finds out at the next appointment. Things get lost — the pet's whole history is a stack of paper that goes missing on a house move, and a dog adopted at three has no record of its first three years. Things cannot be handed over — a kennel, a sitter or a new vet asks what the pet has had, and the answer is a phone photo of a card, or nothing.

Every existing answer to this either does far too much or will not stop nagging. Pet apps bolt on notifications, insurance offers, activity tracking and reminders that arrive at 7am. A handler who wants a written record does not want an app with a red dot on it.

Solution

The solution, from the handler's perspective.

Paw Pages is a written record, one page per pet, that never chases you.

A handler signs up with a name, an email and a password. They add a pet — name, species, and as much of breed, sex, date of birth, colour and a photo as they know. Then they log entries against that pet: a date, what happened in their own words, and optionally the vet, a note, and the date the next one is due. A rabies booster and a nail trim fill in the same form. Nothing is restricted to health.

Because entries carry a next-due date, the home screen opens with a cross-pet ledger of what is due and what is overdue, oldest problem first, with a rubber-stamp OVERDUE impression and a day count on anything past its date. That ledger is the entire reminder system. Paw Pages sends no email and no notification, which is why it never needs a phone number, and why opening the app is the only thing that has to happen.

Any pet's page can be switched on at its own URL and handed to a kennel, a sitter or a family member. The public page shows the pet and the record — photo, name, species, breed, colour, age, and every entry as a date and a title. It shows nothing about the handler, no notes and no clinic names. It is off by default, per pet, and switching it off kills the link at once.

Pets that pass away or are rehomed are archived with a reason. They keep their history and their photo, stop counting toward due dates, and their public page goes dark. Nothing in Paw Pages will ever show an overdue booster in red for a dog that has died.

User Stories

The actor is a handler unless stated otherwise. A visitor is anyone holding a public page link, signed in or not.

Account and identity

  1. As a handler, I want to create an account with my name, email and password, so that I can start keeping records for my pets.
  2. As a handler, I want the signup screen to state plainly that my email is only ever used for password resets, so that I know signing up will not put me on a mailing list.
  3. As a handler, I want to reveal my password as I type it, so that I do not lock myself out with a typo I cannot see.
  4. As a handler, I want to be told immediately if my email is already registered, so that I go to sign in instead of creating a duplicate.
  5. As a handler, I want to sign in with my email and password, so that I can get back to my records on any device.
  6. As a handler, I want to stay signed in between visits, so that checking a due date does not mean typing a password first.
  7. As a handler, I want to reset my password by email, so that forgetting it does not cost me my pets' history.
  8. As a handler, I want to sign out, so that I can safely check a record on someone else's computer.
  9. As a handler, I want to change my display name, so that the app calls me what I want to be called.
  10. As a handler, I want to change my email address, so that losing access to an old inbox does not lock me out.
  11. As a handler, I want to change my password, so that I can rotate it if I think it is compromised.
  12. As a handler, I want to see when I joined and how many pets and entries I have, so that I have a sense of what the account holds.
  13. As a handler, I want to optionally record my date of birth and see my age derived from it, so that I am never asked to keep an age up to date.
  14. As a handler, I want to optionally record my gender and nationality, so that the profile is complete if I want it to be.
  15. As a handler, I want all three personal fields to stay unset unless I fill them in, so that the app holds nothing about me that I did not volunteer.
  16. As a handler, I want to download everything I have entered as one file, so that my pets' history is mine regardless of what happens to this app.
  17. As a handler, I want to delete my account and be shown exactly what will be erased first, so that I can leave without wondering what was kept.
  18. As a handler, I want to be certain no other handler can ever see my pets or entries, so that open signup does not mean my records are exposed.

Keeping a pet

  1. As a handler, I want to add a pet with just a name and a species, so that I can start logging before I have gathered every detail.
  2. As a handler, I want to type the species freely, so that a rabbit, a bird or a tortoise is as welcome as a dog.
  3. As a handler, I want to record breed, sex and colour, so that the pet is identifiable from the record alone.
  4. As a handler, I want to record a date of birth and mark it approximate, so that a rescue with a guessed birthday is neither a blank field nor a lie.
  5. As a handler, I want the app to derive and display age from the date of birth, so that it is never stale and I never have to correct it.
  6. As a handler, I want to upload one profile photo for a pet, so that the page looks like my animal and not a placeholder.
  7. As a handler, I want to replace or remove that photo, so that a bad picture is not permanent.
  8. As a handler, I want a pet with no photo to show a clean initial-letter avatar, so that the record is usable before I get round to a picture.
  9. As a handler, I want to edit any of a pet's details later, so that a guessed breed or a missing birthday can be corrected.
  10. As a handler, I want to keep as many pets as I have, so that one account covers the whole household.
  11. As a handler, I want to see all my pets on the home screen with each one's next due date and last logged date, so that I can tell at a glance which pet I have been neglecting.

Logging what happened

  1. As a handler, I want to log an entry against a pet with a date and a description, so that the thing that happened is written down.
  2. As a handler, I want to type what happened in my own words rather than pick from a list, so that the app never tells me my pet's event is not a valid category.
  3. As a handler, I want the same form for a rabies booster, a vet visit and a nail trim, so that there is nothing to learn and nothing to choose before I start.
  4. As a handler, I want to be offered the entry titles I have used recently as one-tap chips, so that repeat events are two taps rather than retyping.
  5. As a handler, I want Today and Yesterday shortcuts on the date, so that logging something as it happens takes no date entry at all.
  6. As a handler, I want to be stopped from dating an entry in the future, so that the record only ever contains things that have actually happened.
  7. As a handler, I want to record the vet or clinic on the entry itself, so that changing vets does not rewrite the history of who did what.
  8. As a handler, I want to write a free note on an entry, so that a batch number, what the vet said, or how she took the tablet is captured while I remember it.
  9. As a handler, I want to optionally set the date the next one is due, so that the card the vet gave me stops being the only copy.
  10. As a handler, I want interval shortcuts of +1, +3 and +6 months and +1 and +3 years, so that I can set the next due date from the vet's interval without doing calendar arithmetic.
  11. As a handler, I want to say explicitly that nothing is due after this, so that a one-off grooming is not an unfinished form.
  12. As a handler, I want to be stopped from setting a due date before the date the thing happened, so that an impossible record cannot be saved.
  13. As a handler, I want the form to tell me in plain words what the due date will do — appear on my home screen, stay until I log the next one, and never email me, so that I know exactly what I am setting up.
  14. As a handler, I want to edit an entry after saving it, so that a wrong date or a missing note can be fixed.
  15. As a handler, I want to delete an entry, so that something logged against the wrong pet does not stay in the record forever.
  16. As a handler, I want one Save button for both creating and editing, so that the form behaves the same way whichever I am doing.
  17. As a handler, I want the pet pre-selected when I log an entry from that pet's page, so that I do not have to pick the pet I am already looking at.

Due dates and overdue

  1. As a handler, I want to see every pet's outstanding due dates on one screen, oldest first, so that opening the app answers "what needs doing" without any navigation.
  2. As a handler, I want anything overdue stamped with the number of days it is overdue, so that it is impossible to miss and impossible to misjudge.
  3. As a handler, I want to see a summary count of active pets, overdue items and items due within thirty days, so that I get the state of things in one line.
  4. As a handler, I want each pet's own page to open with what that pet needs, so that the ledger is available per-pet as well as across all of them.
  5. As a handler, I want to mark a due date done without logging a new entry, so that something I dealt with elsewhere stops appearing as outstanding.
  6. As a handler, I want a "Log the next one" action on an outstanding item that pre-fills the form from it, so that the common case — the booster was given — is one action.
  7. As a handler, I want the app never to email, text or notify me, so that keeping a pet record costs me nothing in interruptions.
  8. As a handler, I want archived pets excluded from every due date count, so that the app never shows me an overdue booster for a dog that has died.

The pet's page

  1. As a handler, I want the pet's page to be the feed by default — hero, what needs attention, then history, so that the record is the first thing I see rather than a settings screen.
  2. As a handler, I want full history newest first, showing each entry's date, title, note, vet and next due, so that everything I recorded is readable in one column.
  3. As a handler, I want an entry whose due date has passed marked as overdue in the history too, so that the feed and the ledger cannot tell me different things.
  4. As a handler, I want older entries collapsed behind a "show older" control, so that a pet with years of history still opens on what is recent.
  5. As a handler, I want identity, the public page switch and archiving on a separate About tab, so that the settings do not clutter the record.
  6. As a handler, I want the pet's derived age shown next to the date of birth, with the approximate flag visible, so that I remember the birthday was a guess.

The public page

  1. As a handler, I want every pet's public page off by default, so that creating a pet never publishes anything.
  2. As a handler, I want to switch one pet's public page on, so that I can share that pet without sharing the others.
  3. As a handler, I want to be shown exactly what the page will and will not reveal before I switch it on, so that I am not guessing what I am publishing.
  4. As a handler, I want to copy the link with one action, so that handing it to a kennel is a paste.
  5. As a handler, I want to switch the page off again and have the link stop working immediately, so that sharing is reversible.
  6. As a handler, I want the same URL back if I switch it off and on again, so that a link I already gave someone keeps working.
  7. As a visitor, I want to see the pet's photo, name, species, breed, colour and age, so that I can confirm which animal the record belongs to.
  8. As a visitor, I want to see every entry as a date and a title, with the next due date where there is one, so that I can tell what the pet has had and what is coming.
  9. As a visitor, I want the page to be a document with no app navigation, so that it reads as a record rather than an invitation to sign up.
  10. As a visitor, I want the page to work without an account, so that a kennel can read it from a link in a message.
  11. As a handler, I want my name, email and contact details never shown on the page, so that sharing my pet is not sharing me.
  12. As a handler, I want notes and clinic names never shown on the page, so that what I wrote for myself stays for myself.
  13. As a handler, I want the exact date of birth coarsened to a month and year, so that the page carries no more identifying detail than it needs.

Archiving

  1. As a handler, I want to archive a pet and give a reason — passed away, rehomed, or other, so that the record reflects what actually happened.
  2. As a handler, I want archiving to keep the pet's full history and photo, so that I am not asked to delete a pet in order to stop tracking it.
  3. As a handler, I want archiving to take that pet's public page offline, so that a shared link does not outlive the animal.
  4. As a handler, I want to be told plainly what archiving does before I confirm it, so that I am not surprised by any of it at the worst possible moment.
  5. As a handler, I want to see my archived pets listed with their reason and date, so that they are still findable.
  6. As a handler, I want to restore an archived pet, so that a rehoming that fell through, or a mistaken tap, costs me nothing.
  7. As a handler, I want a quiet count of archived pets on the home screen rather than the pets themselves, so that they are present without being in the way.

Getting around

  1. As a handler on a laptop, I want persistent top navigation for Home, Pets and Account, so that the whole app is one click away.
  2. As a handler on a phone, I want a sticky "Log an entry" action instead of a bottom tab bar, so that the thing I actually came to do is always in reach.
  3. As a handler on a phone, I want my account behind the avatar in the header, so that the screen is spent on the record, not on chrome.
  4. As a handler on a phone, I want a layout designed for the phone rather than the desktop one reflowed, so that the app does not feel like a website squeezed into a column.
  5. As a handler, I want every date set in a monospace face, so that a screen made almost entirely of dates reads as data and scans in columns.
  6. As a visitor arriving logged out, I want a landing page that shows a real pet record and states that the app never notifies, so that I can tell in ten seconds whether this is the thing I want.

Implementation Decisions

What gets built, and the calls already made. No file paths — those go stale.

Shape of the system

  1. Two deployables: web/ and backend/ A React single-page app and a FastAPI service. They are the only two things built in v1 — Supabase is infrastructure, not a third component.
  2. The browser talks only to our backend No Supabase client ships to the browser. Not for auth, not for data, not for storage, not for images. The frontend knows one origin and one API. This is a hard rule and the rest of these decisions are shaped around it.
  3. The schema does not change All three migrations stand exactly as written. The database reference said "the browser talks to PostgREST directly, which is precisely why the access rules live in the database" — inserting a backend changes who makes the call, not what the rules need to be. Every table, view, policy and constraint survives untouched. No migration 0004 is needed to start.
  4. RLS stays load-bearing: the backend impersonates the handler The backend does not connect as service_role and hand-roll ownership checks. Each authenticated request opens a transaction that assumes the authenticated role with the caller's verified JWT claims, so all four RLS policies fire in Postgres exactly as they would have for a browser. A forgotten where handler_id = … in backend code returns nothing rather than another handler's pets.
From the prototype — the per-request dependency

The whole mechanism, so it is not re-derived later:

async with pool.acquire() as conn:
    async with conn.transaction():
        await conn.execute("set local role authenticated")
        await conn.execute(
            "select set_config('request.jwt.claims', $1, true)", claims_json
        )
        yield conn   # auth.uid() now resolves; RLS applies to every query

Public-page requests use the same shape with set local role anon, which keeps public_pets and public_entries as the only route in — so a bug in backend code still cannot return a note or a vet name.

  1. Supabase Auth still owns credentials — the backend calls it server-side Sign-up, sign-in, password hashing, reset emails and token refresh remain Supabase Auth's job; nothing in our code touches a password. The only difference from the original design is that the backend makes those calls, not the browser. The on_auth_user_created trigger still fires and still creates the handler row, so there is never a session without a profile.
  2. The session is an httpOnly cookie On sign-in the backend receives Supabase's access and refresh tokens and writes them into an httpOnly; Secure; SameSite=Lax cookie. JavaScript in the page cannot read the token, which is both why decision 2 holds and why an XSS bug cannot exfiltrate a session. The backend refreshes silently when the access token expires and re-issues the cookie.
  3. The service-role key is used for exactly one thing Deleting the auth user on account deletion, through the Supabase Admin API. It is never used to read or write application data. Anything else it touches is a bug.
  4. Photos are proxied, never linked Images serve from our own API, which streams them out of the private pet-photos bucket. No signed URLs, no Supabase domain in the markup. The owner's route reads with the handler's token so the four owner policies apply; the public page's route reads as anon so the "anyone reads photos of public pets" policy applies — which means switching a page off or archiving a pet stops the image resolving at the same instant the page goes dark, with no extra code.
  5. JSON over REST, roughly twenty-two endpoints Everything under /api/public/ is unauthenticated; everything else requires the session cookie and returns 401 without it.

Behaviour the backend owns

  1. The dashboard is one request It returns the due_items rows sorted by due_on ascending, the pet cards with each pet's next due and last logged date, the three summary counts, and the archived count. The home screen is the app's most-loaded page and should not be three round trips.
  2. Due and overdue are computed once, in the database Every screen reads the due_items view. The backend never reimplements "overdue" and neither does the frontend — is_overdue and days_until arrive from Postgres. The archived-pet exclusion lives inside the view, so no endpoint has to remember it.
  3. The public page is one request, pet and entries together GET /public/pets/{slug} reads both public views in one transaction as anon. A slug that does not exist, is not public, or belongs to an archived pet returns the same 404 — a visitor cannot distinguish "never existed" from "switched off".
  4. Slugs: slugify the name, append four random characters, retry on conflict Claimed at insert whether or not the page is public, so toggling off and on returns the same URL. On a unique violation, regenerate the suffix and retry, bounded at five attempts. The database enforces shape and uniqueness; the backend owns generation.
  5. Entry history paginates by cursor GET /pets/{id}/entries takes a limit and an opaque cursor over (happened_on desc, id desc), matching the existing feed index. The pet page loads the recent entries and fetches the rest behind "show older".
  6. Recent entry titles come from the handler's own last entries The chips on the log form are the distinct titles from that handler's most recent entries across all their pets, capped at four. No global vocabulary, no suggestion the handler did not write themselves.
  7. Export is a zip built on request One archive containing a JSON file of the handler, their pets and every entry, plus every photo. Built synchronously and streamed — there is no job queue in v1, and at this scale there does not need to be.
  8. Account deletion removes photos first, then the auth user The foreign-key cascade does not reach storage, so the objects go first. Then the Admin API deletes the auth user, which cascades handlers → pets → entries. Order matters: once the rows are gone, nothing knows which files to remove.
  9. Validation is stated twice, on purpose Pydantic models mirror the database's check constraints — name lengths, note at 2000 characters, no future happened_on, due_on not before happened_on, archive reason from the fixed three. The database remains the authority; the models exist so the handler gets a field-level message instead of a 500. A constraint violation that does reach Postgres is mapped to a 422, never surfaced raw.

The frontend

  1. One React app, two layout trees per screen Single route table, single data layer, single auth state. Each screen resolves to a desktop or a mobile layout component at a breakpoint — genuinely different markup, as drawn, not one tree reflowed with CSS. Data fetching, forms and validation are shared; only the layout branches.
  2. Routes / landing · /signup · /signin · /forgot-password · /reset-password · /home dashboard · /pets/new · /pets/:id feed · /pets/:id/about · /pets/:id/edit · /log (optionally ?pet=) · /account · /p/:slug public page.
  3. Auth state comes from the server, not from a token in the client The app cannot read the session cookie, so "am I signed in" is answered by GET /me on load. A 401 from any call sends the handler to /signin. There is no token parsing, no expiry timer and no refresh logic in the browser.
  4. The public page is a separate layout with no app chrome /p/:slug renders no navigation, no avatar and no sign-in prompt beyond the footer mark. It shares the app's type and colour but none of its furniture — it is a document.
  5. Design tokens are fixed by the design pass Ink #13202E, paper #F2F3EF, rule #DCE0E4, stamp #B23A24, current #2E6B4F. Archivo for display, Public Sans for body, Space Mono for every date. Stamp red appears only on something overdue, at most once per screen.
  6. Dates are formatted, never converted Every date the app stores is a calendar date, not a timestamp. The frontend never offsets or reinterprets one — it formats the date it was given.

Carried in from the scope and design work

  1. A repeat dose is a new entry; the old due date is closed by hand Standing assumption from the scope doc, adopted here. Free-text titles mean the app cannot know two entries are the same series, so nothing closes automatically. "Log the next one" creates a new entry and closes the old due date in the same request.
  2. The public page is unsigned It reads "Shared by Biscuit's owner" — no handler name at all, as drawn.
  3. Overdue is not flagged to a visitor The public page prints a due date in neutral grey with no stamp, as drawn. Publishing an accusation about the handler is not what the page is for.
  4. Every entry is public when the page is on No per-entry visibility switch — that would need real UI on the log form, a v1 cost the scope doc explicitly priced and declined.
  5. Gender and nationality are free text Matching the existing text columns and their length caps. No picker, no list. Neither field drives anything, so neither earns a control.

Testing Decisions

Test what a handler can observe. Never test that a function was called.

What makes a good test here

A good test drives the system the way a handler does and asserts on what comes back out. It does not know which function computed the answer, how many queries it took, or where the internal module boundaries are — so it survives the refactor that will inevitably move all of that around. Concretely: no test mocks the database, no test mocks a React hook, no test asserts on component internals, and no test reaches into a module that is not part of the API or the screen.

The corollary is that a test which breaks when nothing observable changed is a bug in the test. If a test needs a mock to pass, the seam is too low.

Seam 1 — the HTTP API, against a real Postgres

The primary seam, and the highest one available on the backend. Tests drive FastAPI's test client against a throwaway Postgres with all three migrations applied, authenticated as a seeded handler. Nothing is stubbed: RLS is live, the views are live, the constraints are live. This is the only place the security model can actually be proved, and proving it is the point.

What has to be covered here:

  • Two handlers, one dataset — B cannot read, update or delete any of A's pets or entries
  • Overdue is due_on < today and due_closed_at is null, tested at the boundary days
  • Archiving removes a pet from the ledger and 404s its public page in the same request
  • Restoring puts both back
  • Mark done drops an item from the ledger and leaves the entry in the history
  • "Log the next one" creates an entry and closes the old due date together
  • The public payload contains no note, no vet, no id and no exact date of birth
  • A slug that is private, archived or absent returns the same 404
  • Toggling public off and on returns the identical slug
  • Two pets with the same name resolve their slug collision without error
  • A future happened_on, and a due_on before it, are both 422
  • An archive with no reason is rejected
  • Deleting an account removes the photos, then the rows
  • Every authenticated endpoint returns 401 with no cookie
  • A private pet's photo is not served by the public photo route
  • The export contains every pet, every entry and every photo

Seam 2 — the screen, in the browser

React Testing Library, one test file per route, with the API stubbed at the network boundary by MSW. Tests render a route and assert on text a handler can see and controls they can click. Mocking HTTP rather than modules is what keeps this seam high: the fixtures are the same JSON the real API returns, so the contract is exercised rather than bypassed.

What has to be covered here:

  • The ledger orders oldest problem first and stamps overdue with a day count
  • A pet with no photo renders its initial-letter avatar
  • The log form's interval shortcuts produce the correct next-due date
  • "Nothing due after this" clears the due date and the explanatory panel
  • Recent-title chips populate the title field
  • Save creates when new and updates when editing, from the same form
  • Field-level 422s from the API surface next to the right field
  • The feed collapses older entries and expands them on demand
  • The About tab shows the public link only when the page is on
  • The archive panel states its four consequences before confirming
  • The public page renders no navigation and no sign-in prompt
  • A 401 from any call redirects to sign-in
  • Each screen renders its desktop layout above the breakpoint and its mobile layout below

Prior art

There is none — this is the first code in the repository, so both seams are new. They are proposed here rather than discovered, and they are the highest seams available: an HTTP request and a rendered screen. Below them there is nothing worth a dedicated test in v1. The one exception is slug generation, whose retry loop earns a unit test because provoking a collision through the API is awkward and the loop is easy to get subtly wrong.

Deliberately not tested

Supabase Auth itself, Postgres itself, and the migrations in isolation — the API tests exercise all three by using them. No end-to-end browser suite in v1: the two seams meet at the JSON contract, and a third layer would cost more than it catches at this size.

Out of Scope

Q20b is answered — these stay out. Each was proposed, considered and declined.

  • Email or push reminders. The largest single exclusion. Reintroduces a background sending system with delivery, deduplication and opt-outs, and voids the landing page's central claim.
  • Vaccine schedule intelligence. The app knows no schedules and computes no due dates. Every one is typed by hand off the vet's card.
  • Weight tracking and charts.
  • Attachments on entries — card scans, receipts, lab results.
  • Photo galleries. One profile photo per pet, no more.
  • Co-handlers and household sharing. One handler owns a pet; the public page is the only sharing mechanism.
  • Search or filtering across entries. Free-text titles make grouping impossible anyway.
  • Printable per-pet PDF. The public page and the export cover the same ground.
  • Native, mobile or offline builds. A responsive web app with two layout trees.
  • Cost tracking and spend per pet.

Also out, decided earlier and restated so they are not reopened:

  • Video, audio or camera capture. "Record" means a written record.
  • A microchip field. Cut in the design pass; it was the only genuinely sensitive thing the app held.
  • A vet on the pet. Vet and clinic are per entry, so history keeps whoever actually did it.
  • A fixed list of entry types or categories.
  • Per-entry public visibility. The page shows all entries or none.
  • Handlers managing other people's animals — walkers, kennels, shelters.
  • Migrating the abandoned schema. It was reviewed and rejected; nothing carries over.
  • Any scheduled job. No cron, no queue, no background worker.

Further Notes

Assumptions adopted without a further round

Five of the scope document's smaller open questions are settled here by adopting what the design already drew — decisions 25 to 29. They are recorded as assumptions rather than as answers, because none was formally confirmed. Each is cheap to overturn before its screen is built and expensive after: the repeat-dose model shapes the pet page's actions, and per-entry visibility would add real UI to the log form.

Known edges, so they are not rediscovered as bugs

EdgeWhat happens
Timezone on current_date The happened_on <= current_date constraint and the overdue calculation both use the database server's date, which is UTC. A handler in IST logging something late in the evening is up to five and a half hours ahead of it. Accepted for v1 — the failure is a one-day-early rejection at the margin, not lost data. If it becomes real, the fix is to pass the handler's local date into the insert, not to loosen the constraint.
Orphaned photos The foreign-key cascade does not reach storage. Deleting a pet or an account must delete the objects first. There is no cleanup job to catch what the backend misses, so the backend must not miss it.
Two views flagged by the linter public_pets and public_entries are reported as SECURITY DEFINER. That is the design, and it is documented in both views' comments. Do not "fix" it.
A public URL is published Forwardable, screenshot-able, potentially indexed. Removing the microchip field took the sharpest edge off this and the views take the rest — but the property remains.
Gender and nationality are pure liability They drive nothing. They are collected because they were asked for, stored optionally, and named explicitly on the delete-account screen.
The app's value depends on being opened Nothing reaches the handler unprompted. That is why the ledger sits at the top of both the home screen and every pet page, and why the overdue stamp is loud.

Build order

Vertical slices, each a working path from screen to database, so the auth and RLS plumbing is proved on the first one rather than integrated at the end.

  1. Walking skeleton — web/ and backend/ scaffolded, the session cookie, the RLS-claims dependency, GET /me, and a signed-in shell that renders the handler's name.
  2. Auth screens — signup, signin, signout, password reset. Real Supabase Auth end to end.
  3. A pet exists — add a pet, the pet list on the home screen, the pet's About tab. Slug generation lands here.
  4. An entry exists — the universal log form, the pet feed, edit and delete.
  5. Due dates — the ledger, the overdue stamp, needs-attention, Mark done, Log the next one.
  6. The public page — the toggle, the anon read path, /p/:slug.
  7. Photos — upload, replace, remove, and both proxy routes.
  8. Archiving — archive with a reason, restore, and the home-screen count.
  9. Account — personal details, export, deletion.
  10. Landing page — last, because it advertises what by then exists.

Mobile and desktop layouts are built together within each slice, not as a mobile pass at the end. A slice is not done until both exist.

Where this was published
